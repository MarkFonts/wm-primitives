The sixth signal: geom

Same size, weight, color, and case — the letters are simply built differently. Four landings, at 0 · 25 · 50 · 100. The seven roles stop at 45px — the largest size chrome has any business being. Above it is a different instrument: the monster sample. And up there the naming rule inverts. Mea

Ratio √2 — the paper ratio, the one the A-series is built on, so every second step doubles exactly: 48 → 96 → 192. Tailwind’s 5xl–9xl walks 1.25, 1.20, 1.33, 1.33 — four intervals pretending to be a scale. Two of our five steps (68, 96) were already in the codebase.

Leading tightens as the steps climb — a compensation. Tracking does not, and that is the rule: hand-tracking display type is what you do when a font has no optical size axis. Ours has one, and opsz is already tightening the fit at every step. Doing it again by hand double-counts the axis we built the product on.

Grey is a signal, and we were spending it like punctuation. Four levels resolved to 1.00 · 0.64 · 0.40 · 0.24 on our ground — a finer gradation than a page can mean. Three, expressed as opacity on the text color so they compose over any ground and stay honest in both themes.

Three values, and only three: 1.00, 0.62, 0.38. An arbitrary alpha is not an ink. All three rows above are one size, because quiet comes from ink at the size the context calls for — never from a smaller role and a lighter grey.

02 · The corner law

The corner law

A rounded rectangle meets its straight edge at a curvature break: the cap is bending at 1/r, the side at zero, and the eye reads the seam even when it cannot name it. superellipse(1.2) eases curvature to zero before the join instead — G2 continuity, which is how Cal Sans itself is drawn. The UI rounds the way the letterforms do.

The parameter is not the exponent. CSS folds superellipse(1) to round and superellipse(2) to squircle, so the real exponent is 2k — k 1.2 is ≈ 2.30, just past a circle. Use a step or change the design, not the number

The scale is a vocabulary, not a target to migrate toward. A value that is close to a step is the expensive kind of wrong: it reads as the scale to everyone who comes after, so it gets copied, and the copy is what drifts.

Do
  • Sit on a step. 4 · 6 · 8 · 12 · 16 · 24 · 32 · 48 · 64. Every second one doubles, so the scale stays legible when it is read as a series rather than a list.
  • Let steps compose. Nested padding adds up: a 4 inside a container plus an 8 on the row is the 12 the trigger already uses. Alignment falls out for free.
  • Spend the hairlines optically. 1, 2 and 3 are for a ring, a nudge, a bar — alignment, not spacing. They are outside the doubling on purpose.
  • Clear 0.6 × the radius on anything with a round cap, so the letters keep their full height where the shape is shortest.
Don’t
  • Don’t use a near-miss. 5, 7, 9, 10, 14, 20, 28, 40 and 54 all look like the scale at a glance and are not on it. Each is within 20% of a real step, which is exactly why nobody catches it.
  • Don’t tune a number to fix alignment. If two things do not line up, the sum of the nesting is wrong — a step is not.
  • Don’t pad a pill to satisfy the cap rule. Both live pills already clear it. The rule is a floor to check against, not a size to design to.
  • Don’t invent a step for one component. If nothing on the scale fits, the component is the thing to change.

The primitives and the instrument chrome already sit on the scale. Two places deliberately do not, and neither is an oversight: App.css is frozen, and the UI-kit board is a gallery of COSS components rather than house UI — nothing in it aligns against our chrome, so it keeps its own numbers.
