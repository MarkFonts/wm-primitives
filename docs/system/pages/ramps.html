Ramps

One curve, three channels. A scrim's alpha, a blend's colour and a blur's radius are one cubic Bézier applied to three quantities: one sampler, three emitters, no third engine. Every gradient on this page is a string src/gradient.ts emitted; nothing here is typed in, so nothing here can go stale on its own.

The curve cubic-bezier(0.416, 0.657, 0.695, 1)

Two CodePens make this fade by hand: seven stops, each typed in. A cubic-bezier() fitted to those seven lands within 0.0012 of every one, RMS 0.00071 — a third of one step in 8-bit. The fit is measured, not asserted, and it means the clothoid is a preset here, not a code path: the hand-written fade and the curve are the same fade. The white dots are the eight default stops. They crowd toward the transparent end because the curve is sampled by its own parameter, not at even positions — which is where the pens' own stops crowd too.

The edge where a fade announces itself

A straight alpha ramp does not read as straight. Perceived lightness moves fastest at the transparent end, so a linear fade announces itself where it starts and then crawls; on live text that is a visible line across the second row of type, and it is the failure this curve was fitted to remove. The unveiled panel is the control: both scrims fade the same colour over the same text across the same distance, and only the curve differs.

Where you mix the midpoint, four spaces

The received wisdom is that oklab rescues a gradient from the grey sRGB puts at its midpoint. It does not. For the pair above, the midpoint's saturation is 11% in sRGB and 14% in oklab: a straight line between opposite hues crosses the neutral axis in any rectangular space, because that is where the axis is. What oklab buys is even lightness — the red→green midpoint sits at luma 111 against sRGB's 101, so the ramp no longer dips dark in the middle. Only oklch holds chroma, 63% at the same midpoint, by treating hue as an angle and going around the axis instead of through it; it pays with a hue nobody picked. The default stays oklab, the predictable one. The first draft of this page had the claim the wrong way round.

Steering a channel Mass Driver's schema

One curve on the interpolation re-spaces the stops along a fixed path through colour space. A curve per channel moves the path. Left: the same two colours with one channel steered at a time — the ramp leaves the straight line between its endpoints, which is how a ramp escapes sRGB's grey without changing space. Right: the three channels of #F65030 → #3050F6, each on its own axis. R falls, B rises, and G does not move. That flat channel is why the pair never greys, and why steering G on it does nothing at all. channelBlend() reproduces Mass Driver's published output byte for byte; the equality is a test.

Banding 8-bit, and what no curve can fix

Eight bits give 256 levels of alpha, and one step between neighbours is about the same faint edge wherever it sits — 0.365 ΔL* at the dark end of the rows below, 0.351 at the light end — and one edge that size is under what an eye resolves. Banding is not the edge. It is the repetition: steps close enough to read as a pattern, far enough apart not to fuse. Visibility peaks; it is not a slope. The control crosses 217 levels at 5px each and fuses into a smooth ramp. The dark fade crosses 76 at 13px each and bands unmistakably. Push the same steps out to 155px apart, as the previous draft of this chapter did, and the field goes flat and the edges vanish with it. More stops cannot help: an 8-stop ramp and a 2-stop ramp quantise to the same levels and band identically, and nothing in CSS asks for more output bits. Tone matters on its own: the third row has the same 76 levels at the same 13px and a per-step ΔL* within a twentieth of the dark row's, and it is clean. CIELAB is uniform for small patches, not for a one-level edge run across a wide smooth field, which is why every banding complaint is about the dark end.

Mostly you do not need it. Skia dithers a background-image gradient and very nearly does not dither a mask-image: the same ramp measures a per-pixel deviation of 2.98 as a background against 0.22 as a mask. So a scrim is dithered for you, a mask over a flat ground is not, and that mask is the one place in this package that bands. .wm-dither goes after the quantiser, on the wrapper: inside the masked element the noise modulated the ink before the mask touched it and measurably did nothing. After the quantiser is also its limit. It composites over the steps rather than preventing them, so it breaks the widest identical run from 33px to 23px and hides the edge, but the one-level step survives in the column mean, 1.29 against the mask's 0.99, and turning it up adds noise faster than it removes step. The background row is the one that halves the step itself, to 0.49, because Skia dithers before it quantises. The first draft of this chapter said dither removes the staircase; the second said wide terraces are what make it visible. Both were wrong, and both were measured wrong before they were written wrong. Nothing here makes a 76-level fade at this density disappear. The fix that works is a ramp that fuses or a scrim Skia dithers, not a mask across a wide dark field. Judge this chapter on a real display at 100%: a downscaled screenshot averages the steps away.
